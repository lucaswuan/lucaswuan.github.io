01 / Context

Overview

We built Quantum Mic Planner as team primalQuant (Nojan Pourjafar, Jayden Cho, Jayden Wang, Noah Kim, and me) in five days at the University of Waterloo Quantum Club’s 2026 Qiskit Fall Fest hackathon. The next day, we presented it as a poster at the Quantum Fields Symposium.

Wireless mics that transmit at the same time mix and create intermodulation (IM) products, signals at new frequencies. When one lands on another mic’s frequency, that mic picks it up as interference. You describe the venue’s band in plain words, and the tool returns a frequency plan with no third-order IM hits.

02 / Contribution

The work

Why it gets hard

Two mics at f₁ and f₂ create third-order products at 2f₁ − f₂ and 2f₂ − f₁, and three mics add f₁ + f₂ − f₃. Shure notes that most manufacturers recommend at least 250 kHz between any product and any mic. The number of products grows quickly. Five mics make 50, and 40 mics, a size professional shows run routinely, make 31,200.

Professionals use vendor software or an RF coordinator. We built the tool for someone without either, like a school AV technician or a theatre sound designer.

From rules to qubits

Our demo plans five mics on 16 candidate frequencies, from 470 to 474 MHz in 250 kHz steps. Each frequency is a qubit, set to 1 when a mic sits there. The cost counts every set of mics that breaks a rule, so a plan is IM-free exactly when its cost is 0.

  • 15 pairs of frequencies closer than the 500 kHz carrier spacing.
  • 56 triples where a two-tone product lands on a third mic.
  • 252 quadruples where a three-tone product lands on a fourth.

The circuit

The IM rules go into the circuit directly as three- and four-qubit Z terms, 948 in all. Written as a QUBO, the usual form for quantum optimization, the same cost needs 121 binary variables, so using the rules directly keeps the problem at 16 qubits.

We used QAOA in Qiskit. The circuit starts in a Dicke state, an equal mix of every five-mic plan, and its XY mixer only swaps a 1 with a 0, so every sample has exactly five mics and no penalty weight needs tuning. We tuned the angles with COBYLA on a CVaR objective, using an exact NumPy simulation that matches the Qiskit circuit to 12 digits. With three layers, the full circuit has 12,716 CNOTs.

Results

At three layers, 7.1% of 8,000 samples were IM-free, about 13 times the 0.55% of uniform random plans. More layers help, and five layers reach 12% on the exact simulation. The most sampled clean plan puts the mics at 470.000, 470.500, 471.500, 472.750, and 473.500 MHz.

We claim no speed-up. Brute force finds all 24 clean plans in about 10 ms, and greedy search and simulated annealing solve this instance too. Our case for the quantum approach is the modelling, since the IM rules map straight onto the circuit’s gates.

04 / Reflection

Outcome

The tool turns a plain request into a plan with no IM hits, and QAOA found clean plans about 13 times as often as random guessing. It has only run on a simulator, and the exact simulation we used to tune the angles can’t scale, since 40 mics would need at least 782 qubits on this kind of grid.

Next, we’d add fifth-order products, real venue data such as receiver tuning ranges and local TV channels, and a one-layer run on real hardware with a leaner 2,570-CNOT cost layer.
